Parliament has passed the Tribunals Reforms Bill, 2026, a law that repeals the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 and replaces it with a new framework for the appointment and supervision of tribunals. The bill was taken up during the Monsoon Session, which ran from 20 July to 13 August 2026. Both Houses passed 12 bills during the session, but most were cleared with little or no debate, as the proceedings were repeatedly disrupted, including by protests over the NEET-UG paper leak.

The bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 10 August 2026 and passed the same day by voice vote. Its stated purpose is to set up a National Tribunals Commission that will select chairpersons and members for tribunals across the country. Reports said it was passed without debate amid sloganeering by Opposition members. The Rajya Sabha later passed the bill as well, and Opposition members walked out of the House during the proceedings.

The central feature of the bill is a National Tribunals Commission. The Commission would oversee the appointment, administration and functioning of 16 tribunals and authorities. These include the Central Administrative Tribunal, the State Administrative Tribunals, the Securities Appellate Tribunal and the Debts Recovery Tribunal. The Commission would conduct the selection process for filling vacancies in tribunals, review the performance of tribunals, and oversee inquiries into complaints against the conduct of chairpersons or members of tribunals.

The Commission would also develop and maintain a National Tribunals Data Grid, a database covering the tribunals it oversees. Its functions also include inquiring into complaints against tribunal members, as set out above, which makes it the central body for tribunal oversight under the bill.

The Commission would have a chairperson, two judicial members and two technical members. The chairperson must be a Judge of the Supreme Court or a Chief Justice of a High Court. Appointments to the Commission rest with the Central Government, which must consult the Chief Justice of India before appointing the chairperson and the judicial members.

The legislation follows a judgment of the Supreme Court in the Madras Bar Association case. The Court struck down certain provisions of the 2021 Act, holding that they were inconsistent with the principles of judicial independence and the separation of powers. The Court also called for an independent National Tribunals Commission with professional expertise and a transparent oversight mechanism for the appointment and administration of tribunals.

The bill seeks to give effect to these directions. Under the bill, the Commission will be created by statute, which fixes its composition, functions and selection process in law. The Court had said that the appointment and administration of tribunals should be overseen by an independent body, and the bill is the government's legislative response to that position.
